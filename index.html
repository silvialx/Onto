The Project

The project aims to map the main aspects of the debate around metaethics, providing a simple but fairly exhaustive tool both for first-look comprehension and comparisons between authors, concepts and stances.

The work is largely based on the analysis presented by Chrisman in his manual What Is This Thing Called Metaethics? 1, where he identifies four main areas along which different metaethical theories may diverge: Philosophy of Mind, Philosophy of Language, Epistemology, and Metaphysics. Chrisman uses these four pillars to distinguish four major schools of thought: Nonnaturalism, Naturalism, Expressivism, and Error Theory. These broad categories can be applied to a wide range of philosophers and theoretical approaches. Building on this framework, the present project retains Chrisman’s general subdivision while expanding it. Firstly, a more fine-grained classification of the relevant subcategories, with the aim of representing the different schools of thought more explicitly and comprehensively. Secondly, more philosophers not considered in the manual are added into the ontology through the use of Stanford Enciclopedia2 and Philpapers 3.

1 Chrisman 2016. 2 Stanford Enciclopedia of Philosophy. 3 Philpapers.

1. The Process

Gathering information, selecting, categorizing and rendering.

1.1. The material

The process started by reading the relevant material: Chrisman's manual, the Stanford page of metaethics and the relevant books cited by those sources. After that, the list of all the authors and books was sorted. It was then a matter of selecting some of arguments exposed or mentioned in said books. The arguments had to be quite straight-forward in order to be satisfactorily included in the ontology. The claims had to be easily identifiable; the conclusion, if not reducible to a mere support of the position in question, had to be summarizable by a few sentences. This was probably the hardest and most delicate part of the project.

The categorization did not as well come without some troubles. The most puzzling task was deciding how to go about classifying some philosophers who naimly belonged to a particular school, but diverged in one or more of the four axis. Blackburn's quasi-realism is a clear example: although commonly refered to as an expressionist in a broad sense, his minimalistic notion of truth makes his position tangent to realism if committed to a necessary condition. That is: if one commits to a minimalistic view of truth, he affirms that, in a way, ethical statements can be true; which goes against what expressionism commonly holds. The solution was to categorize his thought as distinct from expressivism, and marking his view as truth-apt, mind-independent, but antirepresentationalist. The same Thing was done for several other philosopher: creating ad hoc positions and catogorizing them with specific positions in the different axis, to avoid over-generalization.

1.2. Checking for incoherences

Different positions along the four axis are clearly incompatible with one another. One cannot, for instance, reasonably say that they hold both realism and antirealism: that would be a clear contradiction. Positions that hold such contradictory claims were marked as inconsistent, and consistent positions were marked as consistent. There are, however, more delicate cases of incoherence to keep in mind. one cannot admit that ethical statements are true or false, without claiming that they represent reality, or mental states, or some inference made by the speaker? Let's peak up again Blackburn's example, mentioned above. He indeed holds both antirepresentationalism and truth-aptness of ethical statements. His position is consistent only under the necessary condition of a minimalistic notion of truth. Therefore Blackburn's position and others with similar criticalities were markes as conditionally consistent.

Inconsistency was marked through a simple SWRL rule. Marking consistency, on the other hand, was impossible to do automatically, because of SWRL open-world framework: one cannot infer consistency, using SWRL, by checking a mere absence of incompatibility (isIncombatibleWith object predicates), because the assumption is that, although no such predicates are currently present for the given position, nothing impedes for them being added in the future. In order to still infer consistency automatically in the positions registered in my ontology, a Python code was used.

2. The Ontology

The existing ontologies used and the new classes and object properties implemented.

2.1 Authoritative ontologies

This ontology uses existing authoritative ontologies, in order to preserve usability. Here's the list of the ontologies used:

  • FABIO for bibliographic entities (Book, BookChapter);
  • ARGO for classes such as Argument and Claim, as well as predicates relating to dialectic actions, usually in reference to arguments or claims (eg. negates, affirms, supports);
  • Dublin Core for predicates like creator, which relate the bibliographic entities to their author;
  • SKOS for implementing the object predicate definition.

Moreover, authors and books are linked to the corresponding Wikidata entity through the object predicare rdfs:seeAlso.
